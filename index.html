AVPick predictions on an audio-visual clip: frame-level, multi-label detection of the four classes, so several classes can be active in the same frame. Play the video, or click / drag on the lanes to seek.

Overview

speech vocals instruments sound

Selecting training data for speech and music generation means knowing, frame by frame, which of four sound classes are present in a clip: speech, vocals, instruments, and environmental sound. The video track can help with this, but it is not obvious which of these classes actually gain from it. We compare an audio-only pathway with a fused audio-visual pathway on the same frozen audio-visual backbone (PE-AV), and find that vision helps speech, vocals, and instruments, while the audio-only pathway is better for sound overall; sound sources are also off-screen far more often than those of the other classes. AVPick builds this into one model: two lightweight heads on the shared frozen backbone, one reading fused audio-visual features for speech, vocals, and instruments and one reading audio-only features for sound, with each class's pathway fixed before training. It predicts all four classes on every frame, so co-occurring classes are detected together.

Key Findings

Finding 1

Vision helps three classes, but not sound

In controlled per-class input swaps on SV-Internal, letting a class's dedicated head read fused audio-visual instead of audio-only features raises F1 for speech (+0.67 / +1.24 points), vocals (+1.65 / +1.36) and instruments (+0.42 / +0.40), but lowers it for sound (−2.06 / −2.12), in both settings tested.

Per-class visual contribution, settings A / B.

Finding 2

Sound sources are often off-screen, and vision then adds false positives

Among human-annotated AudioSet-Strict clips containing sound, 44.7% have an off-screen source, versus 5.7–18.6% for the other three classes. On off-screen clips, a variant whose sound head reads fused features (AV2) keeps 97.7% recall but only 27.4% precision, compared with 42.9% precision for AVPick.

Off-screen rates and on/off-screen sound F1.

Finding 3

Head separation and per-class modality choice both matter

On SV-Internal, a single audio-visual head reaches 0.884 macro F1. Giving sound its own head, with both heads still audio-visual (AV2), raises it to 0.902; switching only that head to audio-only features at matched capacity (AVPick) raises it to 0.910. Sound F1 rises from 0.799 to 0.820 and then to 0.840.

Ablation of input routing and head separation (mean over seeds).

Finding 4

Best on all three test sets, and robust to the threshold

AVPick has the highest macro F1 (0.910 / 0.849 / 0.900) and the lowest DER (13.57 / 22.83 / 14.03%) on SV-Internal, AudioSet-Mapped and AudioSet-FrameGold. On SV-Internal, the threshold range over which each class's F1 stays within 0.01 of its best value averages 0.41 in width, versus 0.09–0.21 for four audio-only detectors.

Main results and threshold-robustness analysis.

Method

A clip passes once through the frozen PE-AV backbone, which returns two per-frame feature sequences on a shared frame grid: audio-only features ha from its audio sub-module, which never see the video, and fused audio-visual features hav. The clip-level [CLS] token is dropped. An audio-visual head (4 Conformer blocks) scores speech, vocals, and instruments from hav, and an audio head (2 Conformer blocks) scores sound from ha. Which head scores each class is fixed before training by the sign of its measured visual contribution,

ΔV(c) = F1+V(c) − F1−V(c),

the frame-level F1 difference between reading hav and ha on a held-out development split; classes with ΔV(c) ≤ 0 use the audio-only pathway. Only the two heads are trained, with binary cross-entropy averaged over frames and classes. Because every class is scored by exactly one head, the heads never compete for a shared output, and no routing decision is made at inference. Together the heads have 1.3% as many parameters as the frozen backbone.

Dataset: AudioSet-FrameGold

AudioSet-FrameGold is a human-annotated benchmark for four-class, frame-level, multi-label evaluation, released with the paper. Its clips come from the official AudioSet evaluation set with temporally-strong labels. They are first filtered using AudioSet labels and ontology information only, then selected to be acoustically complex while balancing coverage of the four classes; no model predictions are used at any point of the selection. Every selected clip is then annotated from scratch at frame level under the four-class taxonomy, and the original AudioSet labels are not carried over.

  • Candidate pool. Starting from 16,385 evaluation clips, an ontology-reliability rule removes 2,242 clips and a denylist of 56 ambiguous labels removes 8,941 more, leaving 5,202 candidate clips.
  • Hearing first. Labels follow audible events; the video may help identify a source, but visible sources that cannot be heard are not labeled.
  • Multi-label. Each class is annotated with its own onsets and offsets, so classes can overlap in time. Same-class segments separated by at most 0.4 s are merged, and a lone sound event shorter than 0.4 s is padded to 0.4 s.
  • Quality control. A separate reviewer re-annotates a random sample of every batch; batches below 95% frame-level agreement are returned for re-annotation.
